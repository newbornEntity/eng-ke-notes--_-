## Theme and Introduction

“The Enemy” explores **humanity versus nationalism, medical ethics, moral courage, racial prejudice, wartime dehumanisation, duty, and self-interest**. During the Second World War, Japanese surgeon Dr Sadao finds an injured American prisoner on the shore. As a citizen, he is expected to report the man; as a doctor, he cannot ignore a patient who may die without treatment.

The story does not make the dilemma easy. Sadao does not suddenly stop being patriotic or forget that Tom belongs to the opposing military. Hana does not become emotionally attached to him. They act because a helpless human being stands before them and because professional and moral responsibility becomes more immediate than an abstract label.

## Author

**Pearl S. Buck (1892-1973)** was an American novelist and essayist who spent much of her early life in China. Her work frequently examines Asian societies, family, cultural conflict, and relations between East and West. She received the Pulitzer Prize for *The Good Earth* and the 1938 Nobel Prize in Literature. In this story, the domestic space becomes the setting for a conflict between war-defined identity and common humanity.

## Overview

Dr Sadao, a respected Japanese surgeon, and Hana find an injured white man washed onto the beach below their home. His uniform reveals that he is an escaped American prisoner of war. Leaving him may cost his life; sheltering him may be treated as disloyalty. They carry him inside, and Sadao treats him successfully while Hana assists.

The servants object and eventually leave. As Tom recovers, Sadao's legal and moral problem grows. He tells the old General, who values Sadao as his personal surgeon and promises to have the prisoner quietly removed. The General forgets because he becomes ill and thinks only of himself. Unable to tolerate continued uncertainty, Sadao arranges Tom's escape by sea.

Later, the General admits that he forgot his promise and depends on Sadao's secrecy. Sadao realises he is safe. Seeing that Tom has escaped, he reflects on the white people he disliked in America and wonders why, despite prejudice and national duty, he could not allow this particular man to die.

## Detailed Summary

### 1. Sadao's upbringing and the coastal home

Sadao's house stands on the Japanese coast where he played as a child. His father was serious, ambitious for Japan, and devoted to his only son's education. At twenty-two, Sadao was sent to America to study medicine and surgery. He returned at thirty as a distinguished surgeon and scientist.

He is not serving with troops because his research is valuable and because the old General may need a difficult operation. This detail immediately links professional excellence with political protection.

### 2. Sadao and Hana

Sadao met Hana in America at Professor Harley's home. He allowed himself to love her only after confirming she was Japanese, knowing his father would reject a non-Japanese daughter-in-law. They returned to Japan, received his father's approval, married in the traditional way, and built a happy family.

Their history shows both affection and the influence of racial and cultural expectations. They understand America personally but remain shaped by Japanese nationalism.

### 3. Discovery on the shore

One evening, they see a dark shape emerge from the fog and collapse near the water. On reaching the man, Sadao identifies a serious injury. His cap and uniform show that he is an American sailor and escaped prisoner of war.

The couple immediately recognises the alternatives. If they return him to the sea or leave him, he is unlikely to survive. If they shelter him, they risk arrest and danger to their children and position. They decide first to carry him into the house because Sadao cannot ignore his condition.

### 4. Conflict with the servants

The servants see the decision through national loyalty and traditional belief. The gardener argues that both war and the sea have marked the man for death and that Sadao should not reverse that fate. Yumi refuses to wash a white prisoner.

Hana is initially afraid and shares some prejudice, but Yumi's refusal forces her to see the patient as a helpless man. She begins cleaning him herself. The servants' objections are not cartoon villainy: they fear punishment for the household and believe they are defending the country. Their stance nevertheless shows how war turns compassion into suspected betrayal.

### 5. The operation

Sadao determines that urgent treatment is necessary. Hana assists with the anaesthetic even though the operation distresses her. Once he begins, professional habit takes control: Sadao concentrates on saving the patient and instinctively addresses him as he would any other person under his care. He removes the bullet and stabilises him.

This is the story's decisive moral action. Sadao's medical identity does not erase the political dilemma, but it refuses to let that dilemma be settled by passive death.

### 6. Recovery and growing pressure

The prisoner recovers and eventually says his name is Tom. Sadao refuses detailed information because personal knowledge might deepen involvement. He repeatedly tells himself that the man must be reported, yet continues careful medical supervision.

The servants become cold toward Sadao and Hana. They argue that years in America have weakened the couple's loyalty. Sadao begins typing a report to the police but places the unfinished document in a drawer. On the seventh day, the servants leave together. Hana faces the household and children without their help, while both she and Sadao struggle to decide what is right.

### 7. Sadao tells the General

A messenger from the palace summons Sadao because the General is ill. Hana initially fears that the household secret has been exposed. At the palace, Sadao tells the General about the prisoner.

The General does not report Sadao because he considers the surgeon indispensable. He trusts no one else to operate on him. His protection is therefore not moral courage or sympathy for Tom; it is self-preservation.

### 8. The General's promise

The General proposes a secret method of removing the prisoner and promises to send trusted men. Sadao agrees because it would take the decision out of his hands and protect Hana. Yet as nights pass, nobody arrives. Sadao waits anxiously, listening for signs and checking whether Tom is still alive.

His reaction reveals that delegating the act does not remove moral responsibility. The plan becomes psychologically intolerable even though he had accepted it verbally.

### 9. Decision to help Tom escape

When Tom is strong enough, Sadao decides on a different solution. He places a boat, food, water, clothing, and basic supplies on the shore and directs Tom toward a nearby uninhabited island. From there, Tom can wait for a Korean fishing boat. A flashlight signal will allow Sadao to know whether additional help is needed.

Sadao acts secretly and does not seek praise or friendship. He enables escape because it resolves the conflict without surrendering a recovering patient to likely punishment or keeping his family in indefinite danger.

### 10. The General's operation and confession

The General suffers another attack, and Sadao performs the operation on which the old man's survival depends. After recovery, Sadao mentions that the prisoner escaped. The General suddenly remembers his promise and admits that pain made him think only of himself.

He worries that forgetting might be interpreted as disloyalty. Sadao now understands that the General depends on his silence just as Sadao depends on the General's silence. This mutual vulnerability protects him from arrest.

### 11. Tom's disappearance and Sadao's final question

No signal appears from the island, indicating that Tom has safely left. Sadao feels rewarded by that absence. He then remembers white Americans he disliked while studying abroad and acknowledges that prejudice still exists within him. Yet when faced with one injured young man, he could not abandon his duty to life.

The final question is not confusion about surgical skill. It is recognition that direct human contact defeated the simplified category of “enemy.”

## Character Sketches

### Dr Sadao

- **Highly skilled and disciplined:** his professional identity is central to every decision.
- **Patriotic but humane:** he does not become anti-Japanese; he refuses to let nationalism cancel medical duty.
- **Prejudiced yet capable of moral action:** the story presents growth without pretending bias vanishes completely.
- **Courageous:** risks career, family security, and legal standing to treat Tom.
- **Conflicted:** begins a police report, consults the General, and briefly accepts the General's plan.
- **Ultimately decisive:** arranges escape rather than remain morally passive.
- **Complex protagonist:** his hesitation makes his final choice more meaningful, not less.

### Hana

- **Loyal partner:** shares danger rather than leaving Sadao alone with it.
- **Practically brave:** cleans the prisoner, assists in treatment, runs the household after the servants leave, and keeps the secret.
- **Emotionally honest:** feels fear, disgust, anxiety, and compassion rather than effortless heroism.
- **Protective:** worries about children, servants, and Sadao's position.
- **Moral participant:** although Sadao makes the final escape plan, his actions depend on Hana's sustained cooperation.

### The old General

- **Self-absorbed:** evaluates Sadao's legal position through his own medical need.
- **Authoritarian:** has access to secret violence and assumes power can remove moral problems.
- **Dependent:** his fear of surgery gives Sadao unexpected leverage.
- **Not humane toward Tom:** failure to act results from illness and forgetfulness, not mercy.
- **Satirical figure:** official patriotism masks private self-interest.

### Tom

- **Vulnerable:** enters the story not as a combatant but as an injured prisoner.
- **Grateful:** understands that Sadao saved him and follows the escape instructions.
- **Narratively limited by design:** little biography is provided, forcing readers to judge the moral issue without needing him to be exceptional.

### The servants

- **Patriotic and fearful:** believe the family is risking treason and punishment.
- **Traditional:** see racial and national categories as decisive.
- **Loyal within limits:** leave in protest but later return after Tom is gone.
- **Thematic role:** represent social pressure surrounding private conscience.

## Themes, Symbols, and Title

### Major themes

1. **Humanity above wartime categories:** immediate suffering reveals the person beneath the label.
2. **Medical ethics:** a doctor's duty is tested when the patient is politically unwanted.
3. **Conflicting loyalties:** nation, profession, family, law, and conscience demand different actions.
4. **Prejudice and moral growth:** ethical conduct can exceed imperfect feelings.
5. **Power and self-interest:** the General protects Sadao because he needs him.
6. **Moral responsibility cannot be delegated:** Sadao remains troubled by the General's proposed solution.

### Symbols and contrasts

| Element | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Fog and sea** | Uncertainty; the war's “enemy” arrives without clear boundaries. |
| **Sadao's house** | Private moral space invaded by public conflict. |
| **Father's room** | Traditional Japanese identity becomes the room sheltering an American. |
| **Medical instruments** | Universal professional duty beyond nationality. |
| **Unfinished police report** | Sadao's unresolved conflict between law and conscience. |
| **Boat and island** | Practical moral compromise—escape rather than imprisonment or indefinite concealment. |
| **Absent flashlight signal** | Tom's safety and Sadao's quiet moral reward. |

### Title significance

At the level of war, Tom is “the enemy.” In Sadao's house, he becomes a patient and a young man. The title therefore asks who or what the true enemy is: an opposing soldier, prejudice, war itself, fear, or self-interest. Its definite article also reflects how institutions reduce an individual to a category.

### Narrative style

- **Third-person narration close to Sadao and Hana:** reveals their thoughts and hesitation.
- **Moral dilemma structure:** each solution creates a new conflict.
- **Irony:** the patriotic General neglects his plan through selfishness; the supposedly disloyal doctor acts from professional honour.
- **Contrast:** helpless Tom versus military label; servants' certainty versus couple's doubt; General's power versus dependence.
- **Open moral questioning:** the ending explains action through conscience rather than a simple patriotic verdict.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

1. Sadao's education and father's influence.
2. The couple identifying Tom as an American prisoner.
3. Servants' objections and Yumi's refusal.
4. Sadao's professional instinct during treatment.
5. The unfinished police report and servants' departure.
6. Conversation with the General.
7. Escape plan and absent signal.
8. The General admitting self-absorption.
9. Sadao's final reflection.

### High-value analytical angles

- Why is Sadao's moral arc more central than Hana's, while still depending on her?
- Does treating Tom make Sadao disloyal to Japan? Give a nuanced answer.
- Why is the General's conduct self-interest rather than humanitarianism?
- How do the servants make the dilemma more realistic?
- Why can Sadao not remove responsibility by accepting the General's plan?
- How does the title change meaning by the end?

### Evidence bank for Sadao versus Hana

| Sadao's central arc | Hana's essential support |
|---|---|
| Diagnoses and treats the patient. | Overcomes fear and assists treatment. |
| Holds professional, legal, and national duties simultaneously. | Holds domestic, parental, and moral risks. |
| Starts a report, tells the General, rejects passivity, plans escape. | Sustains secrecy and household through each stage. |
| Final reflection directly frames the story's moral question. | Her cooperation makes his choices practicable. |

### Common mistakes

- Sadao does report the situation to the **General**, but he never completes the police report.
- The General does not forget out of compassion; he is ill and self-absorbed.
- Hana is not merely passive. She performs essential practical and emotional labour.
- The servants' opposition arises from patriotism and fear, not simple personal cruelty.
- Tom's escape is arranged by Sadao; it is not an accidental disappearance.

## Rapid Revision

- **Setting:** wartime Japan, Sadao's coastal home.
- **Patient:** Tom, young escaped American POW.
- **Core dilemma:** doctor/human being versus citizen/family protector.
- **Servants:** oppose help and leave.
- **Police report:** begun but not completed.
- **General:** protects Sadao for personal medical need; forgets promised action.
- **Resolution:** Sadao supplies a boat and route to escape.
- **Proof of safety:** no later signal from the island.
- **Core message:** ethical action may arise not from perfect feelings but from refusing to let labels erase a human life.
