## Theme and Introduction

The play explores **appearance versus reality, social prejudice, disability and alienation, internalised stigma, loneliness, acceptance, agency, listening, and the possibility of psychological change**. Fourteen-year-old Derry has withdrawn from people after an acid accident left one side of his face scarred. He assumes every look contains fear or pity. Mr Lamb, an older man with a tin leg, does not deny difference but refuses to let it define the whole person.

The play avoids the shallow message that suffering disappears if one “thinks positively.” Mr Lamb listens to Derry's anger, challenges selective attention to cruel voices, and offers an environment where Derry may choose connection. Derry's life is not changed by a lecture alone; it begins to change when he acts against fear and returns.

## Author

**Susan Hill (born 1942)** is an English novelist, playwright, and short-story writer known for psychologically perceptive fiction. “On the Face of It” uses stage dialogue, pauses, sounds, and setting to show change in real time. Much of the meaning lies not in action but in how Derry and Mr Lamb listen and respond to each other.

## Characters

| Character | Role and significance |
|---|---|
| **Derry / Derek** | Fourteen-year-old boy with facial scarring; defensive, isolated, intelligent, and capable of asserting agency. |
| **Mr Lamb** | Older man with a tin leg; open, observant, independent, and accepting, though also quietly lonely. |
| **Derry's mother** | Protective but anxious; her pity and desire to keep Derry safe unintentionally reinforce isolation. |
| **Children / visitors** | Mostly offstage; children call Mr Lamb a nickname yet still visit, complicating Derry's belief that mockery always prevents connection. |
| **Garden, bees, and natural sounds** | Function almost like characters by creating an open, living alternative to Derry's enclosed world. |

## Overview

Derry climbs over the wall into Mr Lamb's garden, believing the place empty. Mr Lamb welcomes him and points out that the gate is always open. Derry expects questions or pity about his face and insists that people are frightened by him. Mr Lamb responds through ordinary conversation about apples, weeds, bees, books, and his own tin leg.

Their discussion challenges Derry's assumptions. Mr Lamb argues that one cruel remark should not cancel every other voice, that weeds and flowers are both living plants, and that closing oneself off does not guarantee safety. Derry begins to like the open garden and wants to return.

At home, his mother forbids him from going back. Derry rejects her pity and returns anyway, declaring that otherwise he will never go anywhere. Meanwhile, Mr Lamb falls from a ladder while collecting crab apples. Derry arrives, finds him unresponsive, and weeps. The ending leaves Derry's future uncertain but shows that he has already crossed an important boundary by returning through the open gate.

## Detailed Summary

### Scene One: Mr Lamb's garden

#### 1. Entering over the wall

Derry enters cautiously through long grass after climbing the wall. Mr Lamb's sudden warning about fallen apples startles him. Derry immediately wants to leave and becomes suspicious when he learns he has been observed. Mr Lamb points out that the gate is open and everyone is welcome.

The entrance is symbolic. Derry assumes barriers and trespasses over a wall even when an open route exists. His social life works similarly: he expects exclusion before testing whether welcome is possible.

#### 2. Derry anticipates judgement

Derry insists that Mr Lamb does not know who he is. He says people look first at a boy and then at his face, after which pity and fear replace ordinary recognition. He also admits that he has begun to fear his own reflection.

Mr Lamb neither stares nor pretends the scar does not exist. He treats Derry first as a boy who entered a garden and lets him decide whether to discuss the accident.

#### 3. Weeds, flowers, and the whole person

Mr Lamb draws attention to a patch Derry dismisses as weeds. He asks why one green, growing plant is called a weed and another a flower. Difference does not remove shared life. He then compares their conditions: Derry has a scarred face, while he has a tin leg after a wartime injury.

Derry objects that Mr Lamb can cover the leg. Mr Lamb does not compete over who suffers more. He redirects attention to the whole world, which contains far more than either physical difference.

#### 4. Rejection of clichés

Derry has heard conventional lessons about inner beauty and is angry because they do not change his face or other people's reactions. Mr Lamb does not promise physical transformation. Instead, he says the world changes and remains available to be looked at. His approach differs from consolation: he expands Derry's field of attention.

#### 5. The power of overheard words

Derry remembers a woman saying that his is a face only a mother could love. He treats this remark as final proof about everyone. Mr Lamb asks whether it is the only statement Derry has ever heard. Derry's deeper problem is selective listening: he gives cruel words permanent authority and shuts his ears to alternatives.

#### 6. Bees, perception, and listening

Mr Lamb explains that some people hear bees as buzzing, while sustained listening can make the sound seem like humming or singing. The bees illustrate perception. Reality is not invented by optimism, but attention changes what one is capable of hearing within it.

Derry begins to admit that he likes the garden. It is open, alive, and not arranged around his face.

#### 7. Fear and enclosure

Mr Lamb tells a comic cautionary story about a man who hid from every possible danger but discovered that isolation could not guarantee safety. The point is not to mock anxiety. It is to show that refusing life does not eliminate uncertainty.

Mr Lamb has no curtains because he does not want to shut the world out or himself in. He listens to wind and rain, reads, sits in the garden, and keeps the gate open.

#### 8. Friendship, loneliness, and choice

Mr Lamb says he has friends everywhere and treats Derry as one without demanding personal details. Derry doubts that people really come. Mr Lamb's private comment after Derry leaves—people often promise to return but do not—reveals loneliness beneath his confident openness.

Their conversation turns to hatred, segregation, and choice. Mr Lamb argues that inner bitterness can damage Derry more deeply than the original accident. He rejects the idea that people should be separated into groups according to disability. Every person is both similar to and different from others.

#### 9. Derry promises to return

Derry wants to help collect crab apples but must first tell his mother. Mr Lamb refuses to command him. He repeatedly returns the decision to Derry: only Derry knows what he can choose. Derry leaves, promising to return, while Mr Lamb doubts that he will.

### Scene Two: Derry's house

Derry's mother has heard rumours about Mr Lamb and forbids the visit. Her language assumes that Derry's anger and desires come from his face, and she believes home is safest. Derry now challenges that framework. He says his thoughts, feelings, curiosity, and wish to learn matter more than appearance.

He insists that if he does not go back, he will never go anywhere in the world. He leaves despite her command. This is the clearest evidence of transformation: he chooses movement, relationship, and the wider world.

### Scene Three: Return to the garden

While Derry is away, Mr Lamb climbs a ladder to gather crab apples and falls. Derry returns through the gate, excited to prove that he kept his promise, but finds Mr Lamb silent and apparently dead. His grief ends the play.

The ending is tragic but not necessarily a cancellation of change. Derry returned, opposed fear, asserted choice, and formed a genuine connection. Whether he carries Mr Lamb's ideas forward is left to the reader.

## Character Sketches

### Derry

- **Defensive:** expects pity, fear, or mockery before ordinary conversation can begin.
- **Perceptive:** recognises false consolation and social hypocrisy.
- **Internally wounded by language:** repeated remarks have shaped his self-concept.
- **Angry but not unreachable:** listens when Mr Lamb avoids clichés.
- **Capable of affection and commitment:** returns despite his mother's prohibition.
- **Developing agency:** moves from “people decide who I am” toward “I choose where I go.”
- **Not fully transformed:** one meeting begins change; the ending leaves continuation uncertain.

### Mr Lamb

- **Open:** gate and windows remain open; he welcomes people without records or conditions.
- **Observant:** learns by watching, listening, and thinking.
- **Non-patronising:** acknowledges difference without reducing Derry to it.
- **Independent:** has adapted practically to his tin leg and continues ordinary tasks.
- **Philosophical but concrete:** explains ideas through weeds, bees, apples, and the garden.
- **Quietly lonely:** expects promised visitors not to return.
- **Catalytic:** does not “fix” Derry; he creates space in which Derry makes a choice.

### Derry's mother

- **Protective:** fears harm and unknown people.
- **Pitying:** interprets Derry's emotions through his scar.
- **Controlling through care:** keeping him home may feel safe but reinforces exclusion.
- **Contrast to Mr Lamb:** she decides for Derry; Mr Lamb makes Derry decide for himself.

## Themes, Symbols, and Title

### Major themes

1. **Appearance versus identity:** a visible difference is only one part of a person.
2. **Social prejudice and internalised stigma:** repeated reactions can become the individual's own belief.
3. **Isolation versus openness:** protection can become imprisonment.
4. **Agency:** change begins when Derry chooses to return.
5. **Listening and perception:** the world contains more than the hostile voices one fears.
6. **Loneliness:** both characters are isolated in different ways.
7. **Acceptance without denial:** Mr Lamb neither pities Derry nor pretends difference does not exist.

### Symbols

| Symbol | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Open gate** | Welcome, choice, and access to life; opposite of assumed exclusion. |
| **Wall** | Derry's defensive barriers and expectation of rejection. |
| **No curtains** | Refusal to shut the world out or the self in. |
| **Garden** | Diverse, living world in which difference does not cancel belonging. |
| **Weeds and flowers** | Social labels imposed on equally living beings. |
| **Bees' hum** | Perception changes through patient listening. |
| **Crab apples** | Ordinary usefulness and shared work; reason for Derry to return. |
| **Tin leg and facial scar** | Real differences that need not become total identities. |
| **Ladder fall and silence** | Fragility of time; connection must not be endlessly postponed. |

### Title significance

The expression “on the face of it” means according to first appearance. On the surface, Derry appears hostile and Mr Lamb merely eccentric; deeper attention reveals fear, loneliness, intelligence, and generosity. “Face” also directly connects to the visible feature through which others wrongly define Derry.

### Dramatic techniques

- **Dialogue:** replaces an external narrator and lets outlook emerge through speech.
- **Pauses and silence:** show hesitation, loneliness, emotional change, and the final tragedy.
- **Sound effects:** birds, leaves, bees, apples, running, and the crash make the garden alive.
- **Foreshadowing:** discussion of the ladder and Derry's concern anticipates the ending.
- **Contrast:** wall/gate, curtains/open windows, buzz/sing, weeds/flowers, pity/acceptance.
- **Three-scene structure:** encounter → test of choice → consequence and open future.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

1. Derry's claim that people are afraid of him.
2. Weeds/flowers and tin leg/facial scar comparison.
3. The overheard remark and Mr Lamb's response.
4. Bees as buzz versus song.
5. Open windows and absence of curtains.
6. Friendship and open gate.
7. Derry and his mother arguing.
8. Derry's return and final silence.

### High-value analytical angles

- Why does Derry trust Mr Lamb despite resisting him?
- How does Mr Lamb avoid pity while still acknowledging pain?
- Does Mr Lamb's confidence hide loneliness? Use evidence.
- How do Derry's mother and Mr Lamb differ in their idea of protection?
- Is the ending proof that Mr Lamb's influence failed or succeeded?
- Compare psychological liberation here with **Deep Water**.

### Common mistakes

- Derry is fourteen, and he enters by climbing the wall even though the gate is open.
- Mr Lamb does not claim their experiences are identical; he argues neither difference defines the whole person.
- The play does not guarantee complete transformation after one conversation.
- Derry's mother is overprotective, not malicious.
- Mr Lamb's death is strongly implied by stage silence, but Derry's future remains open.

## Rapid Revision

- **Derry:** 14; facial scarring; isolated by others' reactions and his own expectations.
- **Mr Lamb:** old man; tin leg; open gate; no curtains; listens and observes.
- **Central setting:** diverse garden as model of an inclusive world.
- **Key images:** weeds/flowers, bees, gate/wall, windows/curtains.
- **Turning point:** Derry decides to return despite his mother.
- **Ending:** Mr Lamb's accidental fall; Derry returns and grieves.
- **Core message:** acceptance gives space, but agency belongs to the person choosing whether to re-enter the world.
