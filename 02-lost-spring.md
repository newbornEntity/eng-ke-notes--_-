## Theme and Introduction

The chapter examines **poverty, child labour, exploitation, social inequality, broken promises, inherited occupations, and the loss of childhood**. Its title uses “spring” as a metaphor for youth, growth, hope, and possibility. For Saheb and Mukesh, that season is stolen by hunger and systems that force children into adult labour.

The chapter contains two connected portraits:

1. **Saheb**, a barefoot ragpicker in Seemapuri, searches garbage to survive.
2. **Mukesh**, born into a family of bangle makers in Firozabad, wants to become a motor mechanic.

Both are poor, but they respond differently: Saheb gradually loses even his limited freedom, while Mukesh shows a small but significant willingness to challenge inherited fate.

## Author

**Anees Jung (born 1944)** is an Indian author, journalist, editor, and columnist. Born in Rourkela and raised in Hyderabad, she has written extensively about women and marginalised communities. In this extract from *Lost Spring: Stories of Stolen Childhood*, she combines observation, interviews, reflection, and social analysis rather than presenting a purely fictional plot.

## Overview

In Seemapuri, Saheb searches garbage because his family lost its livelihood in Dhaka and migrated to Delhi. Garbage is both survival for adults and a source of wonder for children. The narrator's casual promise to open a school exposes how easily privileged people make promises they do not fulfil. Later Saheb takes a job at a tea stall. Although he receives regular pay and meals, the steel canister he carries appears heavier than his old plastic bag because he is no longer his own master.

In Firozabad, Mukesh belongs to a caste-bound community that has made glass bangles for generations. Children work in dark, unhealthy spaces and lose their eyesight, while adults remain trapped by moneylenders, middlemen, police, bureaucrats, and politicians. Mukesh nevertheless declares that he will become a motor mechanic and walk to a distant garage to learn. His modest dream becomes an act of courage.

## Detailed Summary

### Part I: “Sometimes I find a rupee in the garbage”

#### 1. Saheb's migration and daily search

The narrator regularly meets Saheb looking through neighbourhood garbage dumps. His family left Dhaka after storms destroyed their fields and home. His full name, **Saheb-e-Alam**, means lord of the universe, but he owns almost nothing. This irony immediately exposes the gap between identity in words and reality.

When the narrator asks why he does not attend school, Saheb says there is none in his neighbourhood. She casually promises to start one. Days later he asks whether it is ready, leaving her embarrassed: a joking promise means hope to a child who has few opportunities.

#### 2. Bare feet and the normalisation of deprivation

The narrator sees many barefoot ragpickers. Explanations such as “tradition” may hide continuing poverty. An anecdote about a boy who prayed for shoes and later obtained them shows that material improvement can occur, yet many children remain without basic protection. The chapter therefore questions romantic explanations for deprivation.

#### 3. Seemapuri: geographically near, socially distant

Seemapuri lies on Delhi's edge but is metaphorically far from the city's prosperity. Families who arrived from Bangladesh in 1971 live in temporary structures without sewage, drainage, or running water. Food matters more immediately than formal identity. Ration cards provide access to grain and allow continued residence.

Ragpicking has become the community's occupation. For adults, garbage is dependable survival; for children, it may also contain a coin or unexpected object. The same heap is therefore economic necessity to one generation and a game of chance to another.

#### 4. Saheb at the tennis club

Saheb watches tennis through a fence and says he likes the game. A discarded pair of tennis shoes gives him brief access to an affluent world, but only through its waste. The fence, shoes, and club mark the invisible social boundary between children who play and children who watch.

#### 5. Employment without freedom

Later Saheb works at a tea stall for eight hundred rupees and meals. Materially, this appears to be progress. Emotionally, however, his face loses its earlier carefree quality. The plastic bag he carried belonged to him; the steel canister belongs to his employer. The heavier canister symbolises dependence, fixed labour, and the loss of autonomy.

### Part II: “I want to drive a car”

#### 1. Firozabad and inherited labour

Mukesh lives in Firozabad, the centre of India's glass-bangle industry. Families have worked around furnaces for generations. Children sit in dark, airless spaces near high temperatures, often damaging their eyesight before adulthood. Beauty in the finished bangles is created through invisible suffering.

#### 2. Homes and working conditions

The narrator walks through foul lanes and crowded houses. Mukesh proudly takes her to his home, even though it is partly ruined. His father has worked hard throughout life but has not managed to repair the house or educate his sons. This detail shows that poverty persists despite labour; effort alone cannot overcome an exploitative structure.

#### 3. Savita and the irony of bangles

Savita, a young girl, helps solder bangles without fully understanding their cultural association with marriage. Nearby, an older woman still wears bangles but has no brightness in her eyes. The object symbolising prosperity and marital happiness is produced by women and children whose own lives contain deprivation.

#### 4. The web of exploitation

The workers recognise their suffering but feel powerless. A younger generation may wish to organise, yet fear of police, lack of leadership, debt, caste expectations, and control by middlemen stop collective action. The narrator identifies two overlapping burdens:

- the family's inherited acceptance of bangle making;
- the larger network of moneylenders, middlemen, law-enforcement officials, bureaucrats, and politicians.

Together they create a cycle that is social, economic, and psychological.

#### 5. Mukesh's alternative dream

Mukesh repeatedly says he will become a motor mechanic. The garage is far away, but he is willing to walk. He does not imagine flying an aeroplane; his ambition remains shaped by what he sees around him. Even so, choosing cars over bangles breaks with family destiny. His direct gaze and persistence suggest the beginning of agency.

## Character Sketches

### Saheb

- **Curious and resilient:** searches for small discoveries within hardship.
- **Deprived:** lacks school, secure housing, footwear, and meaningful choice.
- **Initially independent:** ragpicking is harsh, but he controls his own movement.
- **Later burdened:** regular employment gives income but removes freedom.
- **Symbolic:** represents children whose childhood is consumed by survival.

### Mukesh

- **Determined:** repeats his goal rather than treating it as a passing fantasy.
- **Realistic:** chooses an occupation visible within his world and accepts the long walk needed to learn it.
- **Quietly courageous:** rejecting the hereditary trade challenges caste, family history, and social expectation.
- **Limited but hopeful:** his dream is modest, showing both the reach of deprivation and the power of personal resolve.

### The narrator

- **Observant:** records physical surroundings and small gestures.
- **Self-aware:** recognises the hollowness of her casual school promise.
- **Analytical:** moves from individual stories to systems of exploitation.
- **Empathetic but limited:** she can reveal injustice but cannot solve it merely through sympathy.

## Themes, Symbols, and Contrasts

### Major themes

1. **Stolen childhood:** work, hunger, and responsibility replace play and education.
2. **Poverty as a structure:** deprivation is maintained by migration, caste, debt, weak public services, and institutional exploitation.
3. **Freedom versus security:** Saheb gains wages but loses autonomy.
4. **Dreams and resistance:** Mukesh's goal is small in scale but radical in context.
5. **Broken promises:** society speaks of education and development while leaving children unsupported.
6. **Beauty built on suffering:** colourful bangles hide the conditions of their makers.

## Title Significance

“Lost Spring” means a childhood deprived of growth, education, play, and hope. “Lost” also points to social responsibility: childhood has not disappeared naturally; it has been **taken by poverty and exploitation**. The plural subtitle, “Stories of Stolen Childhood,” makes Saheb and Mukesh representative of many children rather than isolated exceptions.

## Narrative Method and Style

- **First-person reportage:** the narrator personally meets and questions the children.
- **Two-part parallel structure:** different industries and locations reveal the same broad injustice.
- **Irony:** Saheb's grand name; beautiful bangles made in ugly conditions; paid employment that makes him less free.
- **Contrast:** privilege/deprivation, colour/darkness, wonder/survival, dream/fate.
- **Rhetorical questions and reflection:** push the reader from pity toward analysis.
- **Symbolic detail:** ordinary objects carry economic and psychological meaning.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

1. Saheb's name and search through garbage.
2. The narrator's unfulfilled promise of a school.
3. Seemapuri and the two meanings of garbage.
4. The tennis club and discarded shoes.
5. The steel canister appearing heavier than the plastic bag.
6. Mukesh declaring his ambition.
7. The description of bangle workers and the “web” of exploitation.

### High-value analytical angles

- Why is the narrator more effective than a detached third-person account?
- How does employment change Saheb's body language and freedom?
- Why is Mukesh's ordinary career goal extraordinary in context?
- How do beautiful objects conceal exploited labour?
- Explain why poverty here is structural rather than the result of unwillingness to work.
- Compare exploitation in **Lost Spring**, **Indigo**, and **A Roadside Stand**.

### Evidence bank

- Saheb remembers no school and lives in a settlement without basic services.
- Garbage means food and shelter, not merely waste.
- At the club he can watch, not participate; even his shoes are discarded property.
- His paid job brings food and wages but makes him answerable to an owner.
- Mukesh's father has laboured for years without achieving education or secure housing.
- Workers face hereditary expectations and an institutional network of exploitation.
- Mukesh is willing to walk to a distant garage.

### Common mistakes

- Do not say Saheb is happier at the tea stall simply because he earns money.
- Do not describe ragpicking as a genuine “fine art”; the phrase is bitterly ironic.
- Do not blame Mukesh's father for lacking effort.
- Do not claim Mukesh has already escaped poverty; the ending offers **possibility**, not guaranteed success.
- Distinguish the two children: Saheb's arc moves toward lost freedom; Mukesh's arc moves toward tentative resistance.
