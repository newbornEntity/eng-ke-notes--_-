## Theme and Introduction

The chapter explores two connected subjects:

- **Part I:** the interview as a powerful but controversial journalistic form—capable of revealing truth yet also experienced as intrusion.
- **Part II:** an actual interview with Umberto Eco, showing how the form can reveal a person's creative methods, intellectual range, use of time, and understanding of success.

The two parts should be read together. Part I warns against assuming that interviews are neutral; Part II demonstrates their value when the interviewer asks informed questions and lets the subject explain ideas fully.

## Authors and Interviewee

### Christopher Silvester

**Christopher Silvester (born 1959)** is a journalist and editor. Part I is adapted from his introduction to *The Penguin Book of Interviews: An Anthology from 1859 to the Present Day*. He surveys sharply differing attitudes toward the interview.

### Mukund Padmanabhan

**Mukund Padmanabhan**, a journalist associated with *The Hindu*, conducts the interview in Part II. His questions move from Eco's academic style and time management to his fiction and the success of *The Name of the Rose*.

### Umberto Eco

**Umberto Eco (1932-2016)** was an Italian scholar, semiotician, philosopher, novelist, and university professor. He wrote academic works and novels, including *The Name of the Rose*. The interview presents the common intellectual thread beneath his many kinds of writing.

## Overview

Part I explains that the interview has become a standard feature of journalism, but its reputation is divided. Supporters see it as art and a source of truth; many celebrities consider it invasive or harmful. Silvester cites writers who strongly disliked interviews and then notes that, despite imperfections, interviews provide unusually vivid impressions of public figures.

Part II asks Umberto Eco how he produces an enormous amount of work. Eco explains that he uses small gaps in daily life, which he calls empty spaces or interstices. He also says that his academic and fictional writings share an interest in semiotics and a playful, narrative method. He sees himself primarily as a university professor who writes novels on Sundays. Discussing *The Name of the Rose*, he explains its mixture of mystery, theology, philosophy, and medieval history, while admitting that the scale of its commercial success cannot be fully predicted.

## Detailed Summary

## Part I: The interview as a form

### 1. Growth and importance

Since emerging as a journalistic practice, the interview has become commonplace. Literate readers encounter interviews constantly, and public figures may be interviewed repeatedly. The form mediates how society understands famous people.

### 2. Conflicting judgements

Some consider the best interview a source of truth and an art. Others see it as an intrusion that reduces or steals something from its subject. This conflict concerns power: the interviewer selects questions, controls framing, and turns a person's speech into public material.

### 3. Writers who disliked interviews

Silvester assembles strong reactions. Naipaul speaks of emotional injury; Lewis Carroll avoids interviewers and public “lionising”; Kipling condemns the practice despite having worked in journalism; Wells shows a more contradictory relationship; and Bellow compares the experience to pressure around the throat. These examples establish that hostility is not simple shyness—it may arise from concern about privacy, distortion, or loss of control.

### 4. A qualified defence

The chapter does not finally reject interviews. Denis Brian argues that our strongest impressions of contemporaries often come through interviews. The form may be imperfect, but it records voice, personality, reaction, and self-explanation in ways that ordinary reporting cannot.

## Part II: Umberto Eco in conversation

### 1. The mystery of productivity

Padmanabhan begins by noting Eco's extraordinary range and output. Eco rejects the idea that he possesses extra time. Instead, he uses small intervals between scheduled activities. Waiting for an elevator or for someone to arrive can become productive time. He calls these gaps **interstices** or empty spaces.

The point is not frantic multitasking. Eco has trained himself to notice usable fragments of time that others mentally discard.

### 2. A common thread across many works

Eco's academic books, essays for general readers, and novels may look unrelated, but he says they share philosophical and semiotic interests. Semiotics studies signs and systems of meaning. His central concerns therefore travel across different genres.

### 3. Playful academic writing

Eco explains that his doctoral dissertation did not merely present conclusions. It narrated the process of research, including trials and errors. Traditional scholars initially disapproved, but he continued using a more personal and narrative method. This makes his serious academic work playful and story-like without making it intellectually weak.

### 4. Professor first, novelist second

Although the public may know him mainly as a novelist, Eco identifies himself as a university professor who writes novels on Sundays. This humorous formulation clarifies his self-concept: academic inquiry is his central profession, while fiction is another channel for the same curiosity.

### 5. *The Name of the Rose*

Eco's famous novel operates as a mystery story but also engages theology, philosophy, semiotics, and medieval history. Readers may enter through the detective plot and encounter deeper intellectual material. Eco does not simplify the medieval setting merely to guarantee popularity.

### 6. Unpredictable success

The novel sold millions of copies. Eco refuses to give a neat formula for this success. Timing may have mattered, but another book published at the same moment might have failed. His response challenges the assumption that popularity can always be reverse-engineered from content.

## Character / Personality Notes

### Umberto Eco

- **Intellectually unified:** many activities arise from a common interest in meaning and signs.
- **Disciplined:** converts small intervals into usable time.
- **Playful:** brings narrative, humour, and experimentation into scholarship.
- **Modest about fame:** identifies primarily as a professor rather than a celebrity novelist.
- **Comfortable with complexity:** allows a mystery novel to contain demanding ideas.
- **Honest about uncertainty:** does not pretend to know why commercial success occurs.

### Mukund Padmanabhan as interviewer

- **Prepared:** knows Eco's work across genres.
- **Progressive in structure:** moves from daily method to intellectual identity and then a specific novel.
- **Non-intrusive:** asks open questions that produce explanation rather than sensational confession.
- **Demonstrates Part I's positive case:** an interview can reveal ideas while respecting the subject.

## Themes and Technique

### Major themes

1. **Interview and privacy:** public knowledge may conflict with personal control.
2. **Interview as evidence:** direct conversation can preserve voice and personality.
3. **Productive use of time:** meaningful work can grow from small intervals.
4. **Unity beneath diversity:** academic and creative works may share one intellectual core.
5. **Scholarship as narrative:** rigorous thought need not be dry or impersonal.
6. **Complexity and popularity:** demanding art can reach a mass audience, but success remains unpredictable.

### Structural and literary features

- **Two-part structure:** theory/debate followed by practical demonstration.
- **Catalogue of viewpoints:** Part I builds complexity rather than one fixed verdict.
- **Direct question-answer form:** Part II preserves Eco's personality and reasoning.
- **Irony and paradox:** critics of interviews sometimes participate in them; a professor “on Sundays” becomes a globally famous novelist.
- **Metaphor:** interstices turn blank moments into usable spaces.
- **Balanced argument:** the chapter recognises both intrusion and informational value.

## Title Significance

The simple title names the form under investigation. Its singular form can refer both to the institution of interviewing and to the specific Eco conversation. The chapter asks readers to judge the form by its ethics, preparation, purpose, and result rather than label it automatically good or bad.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

1. Claims that interviews reveal truth versus claims that they injure the subject.
2. Kipling's refusal and the irony of his journalistic background.
3. Denis Brian's defence of interviews.
4. Eco's explanation of interstices.
5. The common semiotic thread in his work.
6. His playful dissertation method.
7. “Professor first” self-identification.
8. The success and complexity of *The Name of the Rose*.

### High-value analytical angles

- Is an interview inherently intrusive, or does the method determine its ethics?
- How does Part II answer the debate raised in Part I?
- What do interstices reveal about Eco's discipline and creativity?
- Why does Eco resist separating academic and fictional identity?
- How can a demanding novel become popular without being designed by formula?
- Compare failed communication in **Poets and Pancakes** with successful communication here.

### Evidence bank

- Several major writers describe interviews as invasive or damaging.
- Interviews nevertheless preserve direct impressions unavailable through ordinary biography.
- Padmanabhan asks informed, work-centred questions rather than seeking gossip.
- Eco uses waiting time and small gaps productively.
- His dissertation narrates research rather than hiding uncertainty.
- *The Name of the Rose* combines a mystery framework with complex intellectual material.
- Eco admits that success cannot be fully explained.

### Common mistakes

- Do not claim Part I gives a single anti-interview conclusion.
- “Interstices” are small gaps in daily life, not large holidays.
- Eco does not call himself only a novelist; he foregrounds his academic identity.
- *The Name of the Rose* is not merely a detective story.
- Distinguish the chapter's roles: Silvester writes Part I; Padmanabhan interviews Eco in Part II.
