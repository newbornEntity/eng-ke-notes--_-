## Theme and Introduction

“The Tiger King” is a satire on **arrogant power, fate, superstition, colonial pressure, administrative absurdity, cruelty toward wildlife, and the illusion that authority can control mortality**. A king tries to defeat a prophecy by killing one hundred tigers. His campaign nearly destroys the species, distorts government, and exposes the fear beneath royal bravado. In the end, the “hundredth tiger” is not the powerful animal he expects but a cheaply made wooden toy.

The story deliberately uses exaggeration and absurdity. A speaking infant, ridiculous royal titles, impossible royal priorities, and the surgeons' final announcement create a comic surface. Beneath it lies a serious critique of rulers who treat animals, citizens, officers, and public money as tools of private obsession.

## Author

**Kalki** was the pen name of **R. Krishnamurthy (1899-1954)**, a major Tamil writer, journalist, critic, and participant in India's freedom movement. He is especially known for historical fiction and socially observant prose. In this story, fantasy and humour allow sharp political criticism.

## Overview

Astrologers predict that the prince will die because of a tiger. The ten-day-old child astonishingly speaks and challenges them. After assuming power at twenty, he begins hunting and is warned to be especially careful about the hundredth tiger. He reserves all tiger hunting for himself, kills seventy in his own state, and marries into a royal family with more tigers so he can continue.

His obsession also creates a colonial crisis when a British officer wants to hunt. The king protects his monopoly by sending fifty diamond rings to the officer's wife, who keeps them all. When the king reaches ninety-nine tigers, none can be found. The dewan secretly supplies an old tiger. The Maharaja's shot misses, but the animal collapses and the hunters later complete the kill without telling him.

Believing the prophecy defeated, the king buys his young son a crude wooden tiger. A small splinter causes an infection. Although surgeons operate, the Maharaja dies. Fate is fulfilled through the object he never recognised as the hundredth tiger.

## Detailed Summary

### 1. Prophecy at birth

The narrator introduces the ruler through a deliberately excessive list of titles and immediately reveals that he is dead, postponing the manner of death until the end. At his birth, astrologers predict that the prince will grow into a great warrior but eventually die.

The ten-day-old infant speaks clearly and says that death itself is obvious; the astrologers should specify its manner. Because he is born under the sign of the Bull and the tiger is its enemy, the chief astrologer predicts danger from a tiger. The baby responds with a challenge to tigers. The impossible conversation establishes the story's mocking, exaggerated tone.

### 2. The first tiger and the special warning

The prince grows up in an anglicised royal environment and takes control of the state at twenty. Remembering the prophecy, he begins tiger hunting and triumphantly shows his first kill to the astrologer. The astrologer says he may kill ninety-nine, but the hundredth requires extreme caution. He even stakes his professional identity on the prediction.

The warning makes the king's obsession numerical. Each animal becomes a mark in a private contest with fate.

### 3. Hunting monopoly and misuse of government

The Maharaja bans everyone else from hunting or even disturbing tigers, threatening confiscation of property. The ban is not conservation; it reserves the animals for him. He declares that other state matters will receive attention only after his hundred kills.

This is the central administrative satire: public authority, law, and property rights are reorganised around one ruler's fear.

### 4. Colonial pressure and the diamond rings

A high-ranking British officer wants to hunt a tiger and pose beside it. The Maharaja refuses even when the officer offers to let him perform the actual hunt. The refusal risks British retaliation and possibly the loss of the kingdom.

To protect his throne without surrendering a tiger, the Maharaja orders expensive diamond rings and sends fifty designs to the officer's wife, expecting her to choose one or two. She keeps all of them. The bill reaches three lakh rupees. The king considers the expense successful because his throne and hunting monopoly survive.

The episode satirises colonial hierarchy: even an autocratic Indian ruler must buy safety from an imperial official's household.

### 5. Extinction and strategic marriage

Within ten years, the Maharaja kills seventy tigers in Pratibandapuram, after which the local population disappears. He solves the problem by marrying a princess from a state with many tigers. Each visit to his father-in-law produces five or six more kills until ninety-nine skins decorate his palace.

Marriage, diplomacy, and family visits all become instruments of obsession. The story also shows the environmental cost of treating wildlife as royal inventory.

### 6. Search for the final tiger

The last tiger proves impossible to locate. When sheep disappear in a hillside village, the Maharaja announces a three-year tax exemption and begins a hunt. As the tiger remains unseen, his generosity reverses into anger. Officials lose jobs, and he orders the land tax doubled. The dewan warns of political discontent but is threatened with removal.

The rapid movement from tax relief to punishment reveals that policy depends entirely on royal mood.

### 7. The dewan's secret tiger

Fearing the king, the dewan obtains an old tiger from the People's Park in Madras and, with his wife's help, places it near the hunting ground. The exhausted animal does not behave like the heroic opponent imagined by the Maharaja. It becomes another victim of palace fear.

The king carefully fires, and the tiger collapses. He assumes he has achieved the hundredth kill and orders a grand procession.

### 8. The concealed miss

After the Maharaja leaves, the hunters discover that his shot missed and that the tiger merely collapsed from shock. Afraid of losing their jobs, they kill it themselves and keep the truth hidden. A tomb is raised and the public celebration continues.

This incident multiplies irony: the king believes he has personally defeated fate, the official record supports him, and everyone around him maintains the fiction for self-preservation.

### 9. The wooden tiger

For his son's third birthday, the Maharaja searches for a special gift and chooses a crudely carved wooden tiger. Its actual value is only two annas and a quarter, but the shopkeeper praises it as rare craftsmanship and names a price of three hundred rupees. The king takes it as a royal offering rather than paying, extending the satire of arbitrary power.

While father and son play, a rough splinter enters the Maharaja's hand. The resulting infection becomes serious. Three surgeons perform an operation and then make the absurd announcement that the operation succeeded even though the patient died.

The wooden toy is the true hundredth tiger. The prophecy is fulfilled through chance, poor craftsmanship, flattery, and the king's own choice—not through the dangerous animal he spent his life trying to control.

## Character Sketches

### The Tiger King

- **Fear-driven despite outward bravery:** hunting courage grows from anxiety about death.
- **Arrogant:** believes a numerical campaign can overrule fate.
- **Authoritarian:** monopolises hunting and threatens property and jobs.
- **Administratively irresponsible:** suspends public priorities for private obsession.
- **Politically insecure:** refuses the British officer but pays heavily to preserve his throne.
- **Resourceful but unethical:** uses marriage and policy to obtain more tigers.
- **Tragically blind:** recognises danger only in living tigers, not in his own pride and ordinary negligence.

### The dewan

- **Practical and frightened:** understands political consequences but must obey the king.
- **Complicit under pressure:** secretly supplies a tiger to protect himself and the administration.
- **Comic figure:** his desperate logistics show how autocracy forces officials into absurdity.

### The chief astrologer

- **Dramatic:** phrases the warning mysteriously and stakes his career on it.
- **Function in the plot:** keeps the number one hundred central and represents unavoidable fate.
- **Satirical ambiguity:** astrology appears absurd, yet the prediction is technically fulfilled.

## Themes, Satire, and Irony

### Major themes

1. **Fate versus free will:** the king's attempts to defeat fate become the route through which it operates.
2. **Abuse of power:** laws, taxes, marriage, money, and officials serve personal fear.
3. **Colonial domination:** princely authority remains subordinate to British pleasure.
4. **Environmental destruction:** obsession nearly eliminates local tigers.
5. **Flattery and fear:** subjects hide truth because honesty is dangerous.
6. **Limits of prediction and control:** knowing a prophecy does not provide wisdom.

### Major ironies

| Irony | Effect |
|---|---|
| King hunts to avoid death but hunting leads him to the fatal toy. | Fate works through resistance to it. |
| Ban on tiger hunting appears protective but enables royal slaughter. | Exposes selfish policy disguised as law. |
| Powerful king must bribe an officer's wife. | Reveals colonial hierarchy. |
| “Hundredth” living tiger is not killed by the king. | His victory is based on a false official story. |
| Cheap toy is priced as royal art. | Shows fear-driven flattery and arbitrary economics. |
| Operation called successful though the king dies. | Satirises professional language detached from outcome. |

### Narrative techniques

- **Hyperbole:** royal titles, talking infant, extreme promises, and rapid policy shifts.
- **Dramatic irony:** reader recognises dangers and mistakes the king does not.
- **Satirical narrator:** comments with mock seriousness and strategic delay.
- **Foreshadowing:** opening death announcement and repeated hundredth-tiger warning.
- **Reversal:** feared animal becomes harmless toy.
- **Caricature:** characters embody vanity, fear, bureaucracy, and colonial power.

## Title Significance

The title is the nickname of the Maharaja, earned through his tiger obsession. It also reverses normal hierarchy: he considers himself king over tigers, yet the symbolic hundredth tiger has the final power. The title therefore contains pride and its defeat.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

1. The speaking infant and astrologers.
2. The warning about the hundredth tiger.
3. Hunting ban and neglected state matters.
4. British officer and diamond-ring episode.
5. Marriage as tiger strategy.
6. Tax exemption followed by doubled land tax.
7. Dewan's old tiger and the missed shot.
8. Wooden tiger, shopkeeper, and surgeons' announcement.

### High-value analytical angles

- How does dramatic irony strengthen the satire?
- Is the king brave, fearful, or both?
- How does the story criticise colonial rule and princely autocracy simultaneously?
- Why is the wooden tiger a more effective ending than an attack by a living animal?
- How do officials' fear and flattery sustain bad government?
- Explain how the king becomes the agent of the fate he tries to avoid.

### Common mistakes

- The Maharaja personally kills only **ninety-nine** tigers; the hunters secretly kill the old tiger he counts as number one hundred.
- The British officer mainly wants the prestige and photograph, not meat or conservation.
- The officer's wife keeps all fifty rings; the bill is three lakh rupees.
- The wooden tiger's quoted price is three hundred rupees, while its actual value is two annas and a quarter.
- The ending is satire and dramatic irony, not proof that the king successfully defeated the prophecy.
