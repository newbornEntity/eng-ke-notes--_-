## Theme and Introduction

“Indigo” examines **non-violent resistance, civil disobedience, leadership, self-reliance, justice, freedom from fear, and the connection between political action and social reform**. It recounts Gandhi's 1917 work in Champaran, where British landlords exploited indigo sharecroppers.

The account makes an important distinction: the financial refund secured for the peasants was less important than the psychological result. Once the landlords were forced to negotiate, their prestige weakened and the peasants discovered courage. Gandhi's purpose was therefore not just compensation; it was to help ordinary people recognise their own agency.

## Author

**Louis Fischer (1896-1970)** was an American journalist and biographer who wrote for major international publications. The chapter is an extract from *The Life of Mahatma Gandhi*. Fischer combines historical events with Gandhi's own recollections and comments from people who participated in Champaran.

## Overview

At the 1916 Lucknow Congress, Rajkumar Shukla repeatedly asks Gandhi to visit Champaran. His persistence succeeds. Gandhi travels through Patna and Muzaffarpur, consults lawyers, and investigates the system requiring tenants to grow indigo on fifteen per cent of their land. When synthetic indigo reduces the crop's value, landlords demand compensation to release peasants from the arrangement.

Officials order Gandhi to leave Champaran. He refuses respectfully and accepts the legal consequence, placing conscience above administrative command. Public support grows, the case is dropped, and an official inquiry is created. Gandhi accepts a twenty-five per cent refund because the landlords' compelled surrender is symbolically decisive. He then remains to address education, health, hygiene, and self-reliance.

## Detailed Summary

### 1. Rajkumar Shukla's persistence

At the Indian National Congress session in Lucknow in December 1916, Shukla approaches Gandhi and describes the suffering of Champaran sharecroppers. Gandhi has other commitments and does not immediately agree. Shukla follows him to Cawnpore, Ahmedabad, and elsewhere, waiting for weeks. Gandhi finally gives him a date in Calcutta. Shukla is present at the appointed place and takes him to Bihar.

Shukla's persistence is crucial. He lacks education, wealth, and public status, yet becomes the link between a local injustice and national leadership.

### 2. Patna and evidence of social inequality

In Patna, Shukla takes Gandhi to Rajendra Prasad's house, but the lawyer is away. The servants assume Gandhi is another poor peasant and treat him according to caste prejudice, even restricting his access to water. The incident foreshadows Gandhi's broader concern with dignity and social reform.

### 3. Muzaffarpur and the lawyers

Gandhi goes to Muzaffarpur for reliable information. J. B. Kripalani meets him at midnight with students, and Professor Malkani hosts him. For a government employee to shelter a politically active visitor is unusual in an atmosphere of fear.

Local lawyers explain the peasants' cases and the high legal fees they charge. Gandhi argues that courtroom work alone cannot solve the deeper problem because the peasants are crushed by fear. The first need is confidence and direct investigation.

### 4. The indigo system

Under a long-standing agreement, landlords compel tenants to plant indigo on **three-twentieths, or fifteen per cent**, of their holdings and surrender the crop as rent. When Germany develops synthetic indigo, natural indigo becomes less profitable. The landlords want to end the arrangement, but only after collecting compensation from the peasants. Some peasants pay; others resist and face pressure.

### 5. Investigation and official resistance

Gandhi gathers facts from landlords and officials. The British secretary of the landlords' association denies him information because he is considered an outsider. Gandhi asserts that he is not an outsider where Indian suffering is involved. The commissioner orders him to leave the district, but he proceeds to Motihari.

While travelling to investigate a peasant's case, Gandhi receives an official notice to leave Champaran. He signs receipt but writes that he will disobey. This is a clear act of civil disobedience: open, non-violent, principled, and accompanied by willingness to accept punishment.

### 6. Court appearance and freedom from fear

Thousands of peasants gather at the courthouse. Their presence shows that Gandhi's courage has already affected them. Officials are unprepared for a peaceful mass response. Gandhi helps manage the crowd, demonstrating that resistance can remain disciplined.

In court, he explains his conflict of duties: he respects law but must serve people in distress. He does not deny the violation or seek an easy technical escape. The magistrate postpones judgement and releases him without bail. Soon the Lieutenant-Governor orders the case withdrawn. Official authority has yielded before moral courage and public solidarity.

### 7. Lawyers learn commitment

Gandhi asks the local lawyers what they will do if he is imprisoned. Their first response suggests they would return home because their legal work would end. Gandhi points them back to the peasants' injustice. After discussion, they decide to follow him to prison if necessary. An outsider to Bihar has made them reconsider the meaning of public service.

### 8. Inquiry, negotiation, and the twenty-five per cent settlement

Gandhi and the lawyers record thousands of peasant statements. The authorities appoint an official inquiry commission containing government representatives, landlords, and Gandhi as the sole peasant representative.

The planters eventually agree to refund money. Their representative offers twenty-five per cent, expecting Gandhi to demand more. Gandhi accepts. The exact amount matters less than the fact that the British landlords have been compelled to admit the peasants' claim and surrender part of their authority. Within a few years, they leave the estates, which return to the peasants.

### 9. Constructive work in Champaran

Gandhi does not treat the legal settlement as the end. He sees cultural and social backwardness alongside economic exploitation. Volunteers open primary schools in six villages. Kasturba teaches cleanliness and sanitation. A doctor serves for six months with basic medicines. The work also reveals the material roots of poor hygiene; one woman explains that she owns only the clothes she is wearing.

This section prevents a shallow reading in which reform means simply advising poor people to behave differently. Gandhi's group sees that education, health, sanitation, and material deprivation are connected.

### 10. The lesson of self-reliance

When the English pacifist Charles Freer Andrews visits, the lawyers want him to remain and strengthen their side. Gandhi refuses. Depending on an English supporter because he is English would reveal a lack of confidence. A just cause must be defended through their own courage. This lesson completes the psychological liberation begun at the courthouse.

## Character Sketches

### Gandhi

- **Patient and accessible:** eventually responds to an unknown peasant's appeal.
- **Fact-based:** investigates before launching action.
- **Morally courageous:** disobeys openly and accepts legal responsibility.
- **Non-violent and disciplined:** controls the crowd rather than exploiting disorder.
- **Pragmatic:** accepts a smaller refund for a larger moral victory.
- **Holistic reformer:** links economic justice with schools, health, sanitation, and dignity.
- **Builder of leaders:** teaches lawyers and peasants not to depend on him or Andrews permanently.

### Rajkumar Shukla

- **Poor and illiterate but resolute:** status does not determine political importance.
- **Patient:** follows Gandhi and keeps the appointment after months.
- **Representative:** carries the suffering of Champaran's peasants into a national forum.
- **Catalytic:** without his persistence, the movement would not begin in the form described.

### The lawyers

- **Initially professional and cautious:** focus on cases and fees within the existing system.
- **Capable of growth:** Gandhi's example makes them willing to accept personal risk.
- **Learners in self-reliance:** they must stop using either Gandhi or Andrews as a permanent prop.

## Themes and Significance

### Major themes

1. **Freedom from fear:** the most decisive achievement is psychological.
2. **Civil disobedience:** unjust authority is resisted peacefully, publicly, and responsibly.
3. **Leadership through service:** Gandhi listens, investigates, acts, and develops others' confidence.
4. **Self-reliance:** oppressed people and local leaders must become agents, not spectators.
5. **Pragmatic negotiation:** moral success may matter more than maximising a financial figure.
6. **Integrated reform:** political, economic, educational, and health problems reinforce one another.

### Key symbols and turning points

| Event/detail | Significance |
|---|---|

| **Official notice** | Conflict between administrative law and moral duty. |
| **Courthouse crowd** | Peasants beginning to overcome fear. |

| **Six village schools** | Freedom requires capacity-building after protest. |
| **Refusal of Andrews's support** | Confidence and independence must be practised. |

## Title Significance

The title refers to the crop at the centre of exploitation. “Indigo” also becomes shorthand for the entire system of colonial economic control. Yet the chapter ultimately shifts attention from the crop to the people: once fear weakens, the material symbol of oppression loses power.

## Narrative Style

- **Biographical and documentary:** dates, places, percentages, names, and testimony establish credibility.
- **Anecdotal:** small episodes reveal Gandhi's method and character.
- **Cause-and-effect structure:** exploitation leads to inquiry; official pressure leads to civil disobedience; resistance leads to confidence.
- **Contrast:** fearful peasants versus courthouse solidarity; expensive litigation versus direct service; dependence versus self-reliance.
- **Understated tone:** the account avoids heroic exaggeration and lets actions prove leadership.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

1. Shukla following Gandhi and keeping the Calcutta appointment.
2. Gandhi's meetings with Muzaffarpur lawyers.
3. The fifteen per cent indigo arrangement.
4. Gandhi's conflict of duties before the court.
5. The lawyers deciding whether to face prison.
6. Acceptance of the twenty-five per cent refund.
7. Constructive village work.
8. Refusal to use Andrews as a prop.

### High-value analytical angles

- Why is Champaran a turning point even though the refund is only partial?
- How does Gandhi convert followers into independent participants?
- Explain the relationship between civil disobedience and respect for law.
- How does Shukla challenge assumptions about who can create historical change?
- Why does Gandhi stay after the legal victory?
- Compare exploitation and resistance in **Indigo**, **Lost Spring**, and **A Roadside Stand**.

### Evidence bank

- Gandhi first gathers information rather than acting on incomplete claims.
- He disobeys the notice openly and accepts consequences.
- Peasants gather peacefully, revealing newly awakened courage.
- Lawyers shift from fees and advice to willingness for personal sacrifice.
- Gandhi accepts the refund because compelled concession breaks planter prestige.
- Schools, medical help, and sanitation work follow the settlement.
- Andrews is not retained; the movement must trust its own strength.

### Common mistakes

- The peasants did not voluntarily choose the indigo arrangement.
- The settlement was a **twenty-five per cent refund**, not total repayment.
- Gandhi's success cannot be measured by money alone.
- Do not portray civil disobedience as secret law-breaking; Gandhi is transparent and accountable.
- Shukla is not a polished political leader; his power lies in persistence.

## Rapid Revision

- **Year:** 1917 action; first meeting at Lucknow Congress in December 1916.
- **Key organiser:** Rajkumar Shukla.
- **System:** 3/20 or 15% of land under indigo.
- **Trigger for compensation:** synthetic indigo reduces landlords' need for the crop.
- **Method:** investigation → open disobedience → public courage → inquiry → negotiation.
- **Settlement:** 25% refund; larger victory is loss of planter prestige.
- **Afterward:** schools, health, sanitation, self-reliance.
- **Best concluding line for an answer:** Champaran succeeds because Gandhi turns a crop dispute into a practical education in courage, dignity, and collective agency.
