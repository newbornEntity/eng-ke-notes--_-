## Theme and Introduction

“Memories of Childhood” contains two autobiographical episodes about **discrimination, cultural domination, caste, identity, dignity, resistance, childhood awareness, and education as empowerment**.

- In **“The Cutting of My Long Hair,”** Zitkala-Sa recalls a boarding school that tries to erase Native American identity through compulsory rules and the cutting of her hair.
- In **“We Too Are Human Beings,”** Bama recalls discovering that an elder from her Dalit community must carry a food packet without touching it because of caste prejudice.

The locations and systems differ, but both accounts show power entering ordinary acts—clothing, eating, hair, carrying food, naming a street. Children notice injustice even before they possess full political vocabulary for it.

## Authors

### Zitkala-Sa

**Zitkala-Sa (1876-1938)**, born Gertrude Simmons Bonnin, was a Native American writer, musician, teacher, and activist. She criticised institutions that suppressed Native culture and worked for Indigenous rights. Her pen name asserts cultural identity within English-language publication.

### Bama

**Bama** is the pen name of a Tamil Dalit writer from a Roman Catholic family. Her major works include the autobiography *Karukku* (1992), the novel *Sangati* (1994), and the story collection *Kisumbukkaaran* (1996). Her writing exposes caste and gender oppression while affirming community dignity and resistance.

## Overview

Zitkala-Sa arrives at a cold boarding school where harsh bells, rigid routines, unfamiliar clothing, and an unknown language make her feel controlled. Judewin warns that their long hair will be cut. Because long hair has deep cultural meaning, Zitkala-Sa hides under a bed and resists when found. The authorities overpower her and cut it. She experiences the act as humiliation and reduction from a person with family and culture to one controlled child among many.

Bama describes how a ten-minute walk home often takes much longer because village streets are full of entertaining sights. One day she sees an elder carry a packet by its string to a landlord and initially laughs at the unusual manner. Annan explains that the elder is forbidden from touching the packet because of caste. Bama's laughter becomes anger. Annan advises her to study, advance, and defeat indignity through achievement. She works hard, stands first in class, and gains friends and respect.

## Detailed Summary

## Part I: “The Cutting of My Long Hair” — Zitkala-Sa

### 1. Arrival in the “land of apples”

The first day is cold, noisy, and disorienting. Snow covers the ground, bells ring loudly, shoes strike bare floors, and many voices speak an unfamiliar language. Zitkala-Sa feels that her freedom has already been constrained.

Native girls wear stiff shoes and closely fitted clothing. Zitkala-Sa's soft moccasins and blanket have been replaced. The imposed clothes create physical discomfort and cultural exposure. She is not simply learning a new routine; she is being remade according to someone else's standard.

### 2. “Eating by formula”

Breakfast is governed by bells. Pupils pull out chairs, sit, pray, and begin eating only at designated signals. Because Zitkala-Sa does not understand the sequence, she repeatedly acts at the wrong moment and feels watched and ashamed.

The episode shows institutional control over the smallest bodily action. A meal that should provide comfort becomes a performance in obedience.

### 3. Warning about the hair

Judewin understands enough English to learn that the authorities will cut the girls' long hair. In Zitkala-Sa's culture, short hair is connected with mourning and shingled hair with captured or cowardly people. The haircut therefore carries a meaning completely different from the school's idea of grooming.

Judewin believes they must submit because the authorities are stronger. Zitkala-Sa refuses. Her response—struggle before submission—defines her character and the political meaning of the episode.

### 4. Hiding and resistance

Zitkala-Sa slips upstairs and hides under a bed in a dark room. Staff and students search for her. She remains silent even when her name is called. The hiding place is a last private territory within an institution that controls clothing, language, food, and movement.

### 5. Forced haircut and cultural humiliation

The authorities find her, pull her from hiding, restrain her, and cut her hair despite her resistance. The account focuses on powerlessness and cultural violation rather than treating the event as an ordinary school haircut.

Zitkala-Sa remembers earlier indignities since separation from her mother and recognises that no one in the institution will comfort or reason with her as her mother would. She feels treated as one controlled body among many rather than as a child with a name, family, and culture.

## Part II: “We Too Are Human Beings” — Bama

### 1. A lively walk home

When Bama is in the third standard, the walk from school should take ten minutes but often lasts much longer. She watches street performers, games, shops, food stalls, political meetings, animals, and community activity. The energetic catalogue establishes her curiosity and the richness of village life before the painful discovery.

### 2. The threshing floor

Near her street, people from Bama's community work at a threshing floor while a landlord supervises. An elder arrives carrying a small packet of food by its string, holding it away from his body. The exaggerated care looks comic to the child, who imagines the packet might fall.

The elder bows and extends the packet to the landlord, who opens and eats it. Bama returns home laughing and tells Annan.

### 3. Annan explains caste discrimination

Annan does not laugh. He explains that upper-caste people believe physical contact with Dalits causes pollution. The elder therefore cannot touch the packet intended for the landlord. What appeared to be a funny performance is an enforced ritual of humiliation.

Bama immediately sees the illogic: the food is already wrapped, yet the human being carrying it is treated as contaminating. Her response changes from laughter to sadness, anger, and a desire to challenge the practice.

### 4. Caste hidden inside ordinary questions

Annan describes being stopped by one of the landlord's men and asked which street he lives on. The question appears geographical but is actually a method of identifying caste. Discrimination therefore operates through social codes that outsiders might not notice.

### 5. Education as resistance

Annan tells Bama that their community is denied honour and dignity, but study and progress can help them reject those indignities. He advises her to learn thoroughly and remain ahead in class so others will seek her company and recognise her ability.

Bama takes the advice seriously, studies with complete effort, stands first in her class, and gains many friends. Education does not automatically abolish caste, but it becomes an instrument of confidence, mobility, visibility, and resistance.

## Character Sketches

### Zitkala-Sa

- **Culturally conscious:** understands hair as part of identity, not cosmetic preference.
- **Defiant:** rejects Judewin's belief that strength makes submission necessary.
- **Resourceful:** finds a hiding place and refuses to answer calls.
- **Vulnerable:** feels the absence of her mother and the power of the institution.
- **Reflective adult narrator:** interprets childhood pain as part of systematic cultural oppression.

### Bama

- **Curious and observant:** notices the detail and variety of street life.
- **Initially innocent:** laughs because she lacks the context for the elder's behaviour.
- **Logically critical:** immediately sees the absurdity of pollution rules once explained.
- **Angry at injustice:** refuses to treat humiliation as normal tradition.
- **Determined:** converts Annan's advice into academic achievement.
- **Writer as witness:** adult narration turns a personal memory into social critique.

### Annan

- **Perceptive:** understands both open and coded forms of caste identification.
- **Practical mentor:** offers Bama a concrete path rather than only expressing anger.
- **Hopeful but not naïve:** recognises denied dignity while believing education can create leverage.

### Judewin

- **Better informed linguistically:** warns Zitkala-Sa about the haircut.
- **Fearful and pragmatic:** assumes resistance will fail because authorities are stronger.
- **Foil:** her submission makes Zitkala-Sa's defiance more visible.

## Themes, Symbols, and Title

### Major themes

1. **Discrimination noticed in childhood:** political systems enter daily experience early.
2. **Cultural identity:** hair, clothing, language, food, and streets carry social meaning.
3. **Power over the body:** authorities regulate appearance, touch, posture, and movement.
4. **Humiliation as social control:** oppression seeks not only labour or obedience but acceptance of inferiority.
5. **Resistance:** refusal, education, memory, and writing challenge imposed identities.
6. **Role of mentors:** mother, Annan, and community knowledge help interpret experience.

### Symbols

| Symbol | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Long hair** | Cultural pride, tradition, and identity; forced cutting represents assimilation. |
| **Bells and formulaic meal** | Institutional control over time and the body. |
| **Bed / hiding place** | Last attempt to preserve privacy and agency. |
| **Food packet and string** | Untouchability made visible in an ordinary transaction. |
| **Street question** | Coded method of identifying caste. |
| **Class rank** | Achievement used as resistance and claim to dignity. |

### Title significance

The plural “Memories” joins two individual recollections without claiming they are identical. “Childhood” highlights the early age at which injustice becomes visible and formative. Adult authors revisit these moments because personal memory can expose historical and social systems.

### Narrative style

- **First-person autobiography:** gives authority to those who experienced discrimination.
- **Child viewpoint + adult reflection:** preserves immediate emotion while adding social analysis.
- **Sensory imagery:** harsh sounds and stiff clothing in Part I; colourful street life in Part II.
- **Contrast:** lively curiosity against sudden humiliation; institutional order against cultural violence.
- **Symbolic incident:** one haircut and one food packet represent much larger systems.
- **Parallel structure:** different experiences produce a shared demand for dignity.

## Board Exam Focus

### Extract hotspots

#### Zitkala-Sa

1. First-day noise, clothing, and unfamiliar routines.
2. “Eating by formula.”
3. Judewin's warning and cultural meaning of hair.
4. Hiding under the bed.
5. Forced haircut and memory of her mother.

#### Bama

1. Entertaining sights on the walk home.
2. Elder carrying the packet.
3. Annan explaining untouchability.
4. Street as a caste identifier.
5. Annan's educational advice and Bama's response.

### High-value analytical angles

- Compare the two systems of discrimination without claiming they are identical.
- Why is cutting hair an act of cultural domination rather than routine discipline?
- How does Bama's laughter transform into social awareness?
- Is education presented as a complete cure for caste? Give a balanced response.
- How do ordinary objects expose extraordinary injustice?
- Why are childhood narrators effective witnesses?

### Common mistakes

- Zitkala-Sa resists because of the **cultural meaning** of hair, not vanity.
- Judewin warns her but does not lead the resistance.
- Bama first laughs because she does not understand the reason for the elder's posture.
- The packet is not inherently dirty; caste ideology treats the carrier as polluting.
- Annan recommends education as empowerment, not passive acceptance of discrimination.
- Keep both authors distinct: Zitkala-Sa is Native American; Bama is Tamil Dalit.
